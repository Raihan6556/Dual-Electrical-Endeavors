Resistor circuits, from Ohm’s law to voltage dividers

A study guide for Practical Electronics for Inventors, 4th edition, sections 2.12 through 2.12.5 (pages 51 to 62). Everything in these pages grows out of the four relationships below.

V = I R

Ohm’s law

Voltage pushes current through resistance. More push, more current; more resistance, less current.

P = I V

Power law

How fast a part turns electrical energy into heat, in watts.

ΣIin = ΣIout

Kirchhoff’s current law

Current that flows into a junction must flow out of it. Nothing piles up.

ΣVdrops = Vsource

Kirchhoff’s voltage law

Go around any loop and the drops add up to what the source supplies.

The parallel formula is KCL plus Ohm’s law. The series formula is KVL plus Ohm’s law. The divider equations are the series formula plus Ohm’s law. The 10 percent rule is KCL plus Ohm’s law with one design choice. When you get stuck on a problem, ask two questions: what is shared here (voltage or current), and which of these four laws connects what I know to what I need?

Unit prefixes: just powers of ten

Resistances tend to be big numbers (thousands or millions of ohms), while the currents, voltages and powers in small circuits tend to be tiny fractions of a unit. Prefixes are shorthand so you don’t have to write all the zeros. Each step on the common ladder is a factor of 1000, so converting is always a matter of moving the decimal point three places per step.

The safest habit while you’re learning is to convert everything to base units (Ω, A, V, W) before you calculate, then convert the answer back to a prefix at the end. For example, 0.0000594 A has the decimal moved six places to make 59.4, so it’s 59.4 × 10−6 A, which is 59.4 µA. Going the other way, 2,000,000 Ω is 2 × 106 Ω, or 2 MΩ.

The shortcut that makes this chapter easier

Kilo (103) and milli (10−3) cancel out, so you can skip the decimals entirely for most problems in this section: kΩ × mA = V, V ÷ kΩ = mA, and V × mA = mW. Example 1 on page 52 becomes instant: 1 mA through 4.7 kΩ is 1 × 4.7 = 4.7 V, and 4.7 V × 1 mA = 4.7 mW.

Watch the capital letter

M (mega, a million) and m (milli, a thousandth) are a factor of a billion apart. 2 MΩ and 2 mΩ are wildly different parts.

Ohm’s law and the three faces of power

Ohm’s law says V = IR. Rearranged, it gives I = V/R and R = V/I, so if you know any two of voltage, current and resistance, you can find the third. A good gut check: for a fixed resistor, doubling the voltage doubles the current. For a fixed voltage, doubling the resistance halves the current.

Power is P = IV. Because V = IR, you can substitute to get P = I × (IR) = I²R. Because I = V/R, you can also get P = (V/R) × V = V²/R. These are the same equation wearing three outfits, so pick the one that uses the two quantities you already know. That saves a step and avoids rounding errors creeping in.

Figure 2.39 puts a 100 Ω resistor across a 12 V battery. The current is 12 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.12 A = 120 mA. The power is 0.12 A × 12 V = 1.44 W. You get the same 1.44 W from I²R (0.0144 × 100) and from V²/R (144 ÷ 100), which is a nice way to check your work.

Power ratings: don’t cook the resistor

Every real resistor has a maximum power it can safely turn into heat. Go past it and the resistor overheats, its internal structure gets damaged, its resistance drifts, and eventually it smokes. Ordinary through-hole resistors come in ⅛, ¼, ½ and 1 W ratings. Power resistors go from 2 W up to several hundred watts. (Surface-mount parts are often smaller still: a tiny 0402 chip resistor is commonly rated around 1/16 W.)

The rule of thumb is to pick a rating at least twice the power you expect. In Figure 2.39 the resistor dissipates 1.44 W, so twice that is 2.88 W. A 2 W part would survive, but a 3 W part is the one that follows the rule and gives you a safety margin for heat, tolerances and surprises.

Figure 2.40 teaches the counterintuitive part. With the voltage fixed, P = V²/R, so a smaller resistance means more power. At 5 V, a 200 Ω resistor dissipates 0.125 W, a 100 Ω dissipates 0.25 W, a 50 Ω dissipates 0.5 W, and a 25 Ω dissipates a full 1 W. Lower the resistance and the current climbs, and it climbs faster than the voltage-times-current product shrinks. That’s why a small resistor placed straight across a supply is the one that melts.

Example 4, page 52: the highest voltage each 1 W resistor can take

Extra beyond the book

Real resistors also have a maximum working voltage, often 200 to 500 V for small through-hole parts. So the 1 MΩ resistor would hit its voltage limit (and could arc) long before it reached 1 W at 1000 V. For the low-value resistors, power is the limit; for very high values, voltage usually is.

Resistors in parallel: same voltage, split current

Resistors are in parallel when both ends of each one connect to the same two nodes. That means every resistor sees exactly the same voltage. The current, on the other hand, splits up between the branches, and more of it goes through the smaller resistance because that path is easier.

Every branch you add is another path for current, so the total resistance goes down. The total is always lower than the smallest resistor in the group. That gives you a free sanity check: if you put 1 kΩ and 3 kΩ in parallel and get anything above 1 kΩ, something went wrong.

1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + …(Eq. 2.17, any number of resistors)
Rtotal = R1 × R2R1 + R2(Eq. 2.18, exactly two resistors: “product over sum”)

Where the formula comes from. By KCL, the total current is the sum of the branch currents: Itotal = I1 + I2 + I3 + …. By Ohm’s law, each branch current is V/Rn, and V is the same for all of them. So Itotal = V × (1/R1 + 1/R2 + …). The whole combination also obeys Ohm’s law, Itotal = V/Rtotal, so the bracket must equal 1/Rtotal. (The book says the bracket is Rtotal, but it’s really the reciprocal. See the book typos section.)

The || shorthand. R1 || R2 means “R1 in parallel with R2.” Treat || like multiplication in the order of operations: in Z = R1 + R2 || Rload, you do R2 || Rload first, then add R1.

Shortcuts worth knowing

Two equal resistors in parallel give half: 10 kΩ || 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ. In general, n equal resistors give R/n. If one resistor is ten or more times bigger than the other, the total is just a bit under the smaller one: 1 kΩ || 1 MΩ ≈ 999 Ω.

The current divider

I1 = Iin × R2R1 + R2    I2 = Iin × R1R1 + R2

Use these when you know the total current going into a parallel pair but not the voltage. Notice that the other resistor goes on top. That looks backwards at first, but it makes sense: if R2 is enormous, almost no current goes through it, so nearly all of Iin goes through R1, and the formula gives I1 ≈ Iin. Check it on the example: 16 mA × 3k/(1k + 3k) = 12 mA. ✓

Power in parallel. Since every resistor has the same V, use P = V²/R. The smallest resistor carries the most current and dissipates the most power, so it’s the one that needs the biggest rating.

Example 1, page 53: 1 kΩ || 3 kΩ on 12 V

  1. Rtotal = (1000 × 3000) ÷ (1000 + 3000) = 3,000,000 ÷ 4000 = 750 Ω
  2. I1 = 12 V ÷ 1 kΩ = 12 mA, I2 = 12 V ÷ 3 kΩ = 4 mA
  3. KCL: Iin = 12 + 4 = 16 mA (check: 12 V ÷ 750 Ω = 16 mA ✓)
  4. P1 = 12 mA × 12 V = 144 mW, P2 = 4 mA × 12 V = 48 mW, total 192 mW

Example 2, page 54: 1 kΩ, 2 kΩ and 4 kΩ in parallel on 24 V

  1. 1/Rtotal = 1/1000 + 1/2000 + 1/4000 = 0.001 + 0.0005 + 0.00025 = 0.00175 Ω−1
  2. Rtotal = 1 ÷ 0.00175 = 571 Ω (the book rounds to 572)
  3. Branch currents: 24 V ÷ 1k = 24 mA, 24 V ÷ 2k = 12 mA, 24 V ÷ 4k = 6 mA
  4. KCL: Itotal = 24 + 12 + 6 = 42 mA
  5. Powers: 24 V × 24 mA = 576 mW, 24 V × 12 mA = 288 mW, 24 V × 6 mA = 144 mW, total ≈ 1.0 W

Notice the pattern: each time the resistance doubles, the current and power halve. Also, by the 2× rule R1 should really be rated above 1.15 W, so the 1 W part in Figure 2.42 is cutting it close.

Resistors in series: same current, split voltage

Resistors are in series when they’re connected end to end with nothing else branching off between them. There’s only one path, so the same current flows through every one. The voltage is what splits up, and each resistor gets a share proportional to its resistance.

The total resistance is simply the sum, Rtotal = R1 + R2 + R3 + … (Eq. 2.19), and it’s always bigger than the largest resistor. The derivation is the mirror image of the parallel one: by KVL, Vtotal = V1 + V2 + …; each drop is I × Rn with the same I; so I × Rtotal = I × R1 + I × R2 + …, and the I’s cancel.

The voltage divider

V1 = Vin × R1R1 + R2    V2 = Vout = Vin × R2R1 + R2

These come from plugging I = Vin/(R1 + R2) into V = IR for each resistor. The book says they’re worth memorizing, and they are. Unlike the current divider, here the same resistor goes on top: a bigger resistor gets a bigger share of the voltage. The 2 kΩ resistor gets twice the voltage of the 1 kΩ one (6 V versus 3 V), and together they add back up to the 9 V supply.

Power in series. Every resistor has the same I, so use P = I²R. The largest resistor dissipates the most power, which is the opposite of the parallel case. In the example, the 2 kΩ dissipates 18 mW and the 1 kΩ dissipates 9 mW.

Designing a voltage divider, and why loads ruin naive ones

The unloaded case. If whatever you connect to Vout draws almost no current (Example 2 uses an IC with a 10 MΩ input), you can use the divider equation directly. Pick R2, then rearrange for R1. With a 9 V supply, a 5 V target and R2 = 10 kΩ, R1 = 10 kΩ × (9 − 5) ÷ 5 = 8 kΩ. The rearranged formula is worth having on your sheet:

R1 = R2 × Vin − VoutVout

The catch. A load connected across R2 is another resistor in parallel with R2. Parallel combinations are always smaller, so the bottom half of the divider effectively shrinks and Vout drops. Example 3 shows how bad this gets. A 3 V device drawing 9.1 mA behaves like a 3 V ÷ 9.1 mA ≈ 330 Ω resistor. A naive 10 V to 3 V divider of R1 = 7 kΩ and R2 = 3 kΩ gives exactly 3 V with nothing attached, but plug the device in and the bottom becomes 3 kΩ || 330 Ω ≈ 297 Ω. Now Vout = 10 × 297 ÷ (7000 + 297) ≈ 0.41 V. The device won’t even turn on.

The 10 percent rule fixes this by designing the divider around the load current instead of ignoring it. You deliberately let a small extra current, the bleeder current, flow through R2, equal to 10 percent of the load current. Then R1 is sized to carry the bleeder current plus the load current while dropping the leftover voltage. Larger bleeder currents make the output stiffer (less sensitive to load changes) but waste more power; 10 percent is the textbook compromise.

  1. Bleeder current: Ibleed = 0.10 × Iload
  2. Bleeder resistor: R2 = Vout ÷ Ibleed, then pick the nearest standard value
  3. Current through R1: I1 = Ibleed + Iload (KCL at the output node)
  4. Top resistor: R1 = (Vin − Vout) ÷ I1
  5. Power ratings: P = V²/R for each, then apply the 2× rule

Example 3, page 57: 10 V supply, 3 V load at 9.1 mA

  1. Ibleed = 0.10 × 9.1 mA = 0.91 mA
  2. R2 = 3 V ÷ 0.91 mA = 3297 Ω, so use the standard 3.3 kΩ
  3. I1 = 0.91 + 9.1 = 10.0 mA
  4. R1 = (10 − 3) V ÷ 10 mA = 700 Ω
  5. PR1 = 7² ÷ 700 = 70 mW, PR2 = 3² ÷ 3300 ≈ 2.7 mW, so ¼ W parts are plenty

After you swap in a standard value, recompute the bleeder current with the real resistor (3 V ÷ 3300 Ω = 0.909 mA here) so your numbers stay honest. Page 58 also notes that if a calculation lands on 500 Ω, a standard 510 Ω (5 percent) resistor is fine.

Reducing a complex resistor network

Most real circuits mix series and parallel. The method is to find a pair you can clearly identify as series or parallel, replace it with one equivalent resistor, redraw, and repeat until a single resistor is left. Then you “unfold” the circuit in reverse to find every individual voltage and current. It feels long at first, but every single step is just one of the two formulas you already know.

The hardest part is spotting what’s in series and what’s in parallel. Two resistors are in series only if the same current must flow through both, meaning nothing else connects at the node between them. Two resistors are in parallel if both of their ends land on the same two nodes. Labeling the nodes (a, b, c, d like the book does) makes this much easier to see.

  1. Label every node. Find the innermost group that is purely series or purely parallel.
  2. Replace it with Req and redraw. Repeat until one resistor remains.
  3. Total current: Itotal = Vsource ÷ Req(final).
  4. Work backwards: in a series chain, the current is the same, so V = I × R for each part. In a parallel group, the voltage is the same, so I = V ÷ R for each branch.
  5. Check with KCL (currents at a junction add up) and KVL (drops around a loop add up to the source).

Example 1, page 59

  1. R2 || R3 = (10 × 8) ÷ (10 + 8) = 4.44 kΩ
  2. Add R1 in series: 5.0 + 4.44 = 9.44 kΩ
  3. Itotal = 250 V ÷ 9.44 kΩ = 26.5 mA (this is I1)
  4. V across the pair = 26.5 mA × 4.44 kΩ = 118 V = V2 = V3
  5. I2 = 118 ÷ 10k = 11.8 mA, I3 = 118 ÷ 8k = 14.7 mA
  6. V1 = 250 − 118 = 132 V (KVL)

The book gets 26.6 mA, 117 V and 133 V because it rounded 4.44 to 4.4 early. Lesson: carry an extra digit until the final answer.

Example 2, page 60, with corrected numbers

  1. R3 + R4 = 3.3 + 10 = 13.3 kΩ
  2. R2 || 13.3k = (6.8 × 13.3) ÷ (6.8 + 13.3) = 90.44 ÷ 20.1 = 4.50 kΩ
  3. Add R1: 1.0 + 4.50 = 5.50 kΩ
  4. Itotal = 12 V ÷ 5.50 kΩ = 2.18 mA
  5. Vb = 2.18 mA × 4.50 kΩ = 9.82 V; VR1 = 12 − 9.82 = 2.18 V
  6. I2 = 9.82 ÷ 6.8k = 1.44 mA; I3 = I4 = 9.82 ÷ 13.3k = 0.738 mA (KCL: 1.44 + 0.74 = 2.18 ✓)
  7. VR3 = 0.738 × 3.3 = 2.44 V; VR4 = 0.738 × 10 = 7.38 V (KVL: 2.44 + 7.38 = 9.82 ✓)

The book writes 4.3 kΩ in step 2, which is an arithmetic slip, and every number after it inherits the error. The method in the book is right; only the values are off.

Multiple voltage dividers

A multiple divider is one long series string of resistors with “taps” at the voltages you need, each feeding its own load. You design it from the bottom (ground) upward. The bleeder resistor at the bottom carries 10 percent of the total load current, not 10 percent of any single load. Every resistor above it carries the bleeder current plus the current of every load tapped off below it, because that current has to come down through the string to reach those taps. Each resistor’s value is the voltage between its two taps divided by its current.

In Example 1 the loads are 75 V at 30 mA, 50 V at 10 mA and 25 V at 10 mA on a 100 V supply. The total load current is 50 mA, so the bleeder is 5 mA. Climbing up the string, each resistor picks up the load just below it, and every resistor in this design happens to drop 25 V:

In Figure 2.50 the string spans 100 V with ground placed so the outputs are +50 V, +25 V and −25 V. Why dividers aren’t the end of the story

In Example 2 the supply delivers about 11.6 W, the loads use 5.25 W, and the resistors waste roughly 6.35 W, more than half. Dividers are also unregulated: if a load changes its current or the supply voltage wanders, every output shifts (try the “load halves” preset in the simulator above). For steady voltages at real currents, the book points ahead to voltage regulator ICs. Section 2.13 starts that story with the ideal voltage source, which holds its voltage fixed no matter how much current the load demands.

Summary of the reading

The whole section in a few paragraphs

Section 2.12 is about what resistors do in circuits and how to calculate it. It opens with unit prefixes, since resistances are usually large (kΩ, MΩ) and currents, voltages and powers usually small (mA, µA, mW). Ohm’s law (V = IR) and the power law (P = IV, which also becomes I²R and V²/R) let you find any quantity from two others. Every resistor has a power rating, and you should pick one at least twice the power you expect. At a fixed voltage, smaller resistors dissipate more power, which is why they burn up.

Parallel resistors share the same voltage and split the current; their total is smaller than the smallest one (1/Rtotal = Σ1/R, or product over sum for two). Series resistors share the same current and split the voltage; their total is the sum. Both rules come from combining Ohm’s law with Kirchhoff’s laws, and they lead to the current divider and voltage divider equations. A voltage divider only works as calculated when nothing draws current from it. Once a load is attached it acts like a resistor in parallel with R2 and drags the output down, so the 10 percent rule sizes the bleeder resistor for 10 percent of the load current and sizes R1 for the bleeder plus the load.

Complex networks get solved by repeatedly collapsing series and parallel groups into single equivalents, finding the total current, then working back out. Multiple-output dividers stack the same idea, working from ground upward with a bleeder of 10 percent of the total load, and moving the ground point gives positive and negative outputs from one supply. The section ends by noting that dividers are wasteful and unregulated, setting up voltage sources and regulators next.
